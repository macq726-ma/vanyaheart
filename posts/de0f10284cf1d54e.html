How to Choose a Preservation System: Balancing Mildness and Efficacy

This article answers: How do you choose a preservation system? How do you balance mildness and efficacy?

First the conclusion, then the mechanism, and finally the verification actions you can reproduce yourself. This way, even if you switch suppliers, this framework still holds.

I. The Direct Answer

Let me give the answer straight. Selection doesn't rely on preference; it relies on boundary conditions. First, lock down this boundary: total aerobic microbial count limit of 500 CFU/g, mold and yeast limit of 100 CFU/g, and heat-resistant coliforms, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa must not be detected, per the *Cosmetic Safety Technical Specifications*. Then screen your options against this boundary — an option outside the boundary will pay a price on another dimension even if a single metric looks better, and the overall tally won't be worth it.

II. Mechanism: Why It Works This Way

To understand this phenomenon, you first need to look at how the system is built.

First, most organic acid preservatives rely on the undissociated form to be effective; as pH rises, the dissociation ratio increases and antimicrobial power drops. The effective pH window for these systems is typically below 5.5; above that, you need to switch systems rather than increase dosage.

Second, the preservative challenge test is conducted per ISO 11930, inoculating five strains total — bacteria, yeast, and mold — with sampling and counting on days 7, 14, and 28. The judgment is based on the log reduction in microbial count, not on "whether anything grew."

Third, thickener selection depends on the system's ionic strength and pH: carbomer types need to be neutralized into a salt form to build a backbone, and viscosity drops noticeably in high-electrolyte formulas; xanthan gum resists electrolytes but has strong thixotropy and feels tacky on skin.

One boundary case to add: the effectiveness of a preservation system depends on the partition coefficient, not the added amount. Only the free form of a preservative has antimicrobial activity; once solubilized by surfactant micelles or the oil phase, activity drops substantially, so high-surfactant formulas need a higher apparent addition level. This point determines how the formula performs under extreme conditions and should be verified at the sample development stage.

The judgment criteria are verifiable; the key is knowing which item to check.

Total aerobic microbial count limit of 500 CFU/g, mold and yeast limit of 100 CFU/g, and heat-resistant coliforms, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa must not be detected, per the *Cosmetic Safety Technical Specifications*.

Type testing must be performed at least once a year on the full panel; routine factory release testing covers only total aerobic microbial count, mold and yeast, pH, appearance, and net content. Treating factory release testing as a full panel multiplies cost several times over and is unnecessary.

pH measurement requires a calibrated combination electrode, and the sample temperature must be held constant near 25°C; inserting the electrode directly into cream samples easily causes liquid junction potential drift, so the standard practice is to measure after dilution or use a dedicated flat-tip electrode.

Preservative efficacy testing is judged per ISO 11930: bacteria must show no less than a 3-log reduction by day 14 and no rebound by day 28; yeast and mold must show no less than a 1-log reduction. Falling short means the system is inadequate.

One more item that's easily overlooked: routine testing items follow four groups — "five microbial tests + four heavy metals + stability + type testing"; for export, add the preservative challenge test and eye irritation, with the specific items determined by the union of the product's execution standard and the importing country's regulations. This doesn't directly determine whether the finished product is good or bad, but it determines whether problems can be traced.

IV. Common Misconceptions

The three below are all from real projects.

Assuming that less preservative means milder — in reality, microbial contamination caused by insufficient preservation harms skin far more than preservatives used in compliance; what matters is system efficacy, not the absolute value of the added amount.

Assuming "preservative-free" means truly no preservative ingredients — most products claiming to be preservative-free use polyols or organic acids, which still serve a preservative function, just not on the restricted list; the claim language and the formula substance must be viewed separately.

V. How to Verify

Verification doesn't require special equipment; just work through the following items one by one.

Step one: determine whether the preservation system is adequate by running a challenge test and checking the log reduction in microbial count at day 28 — bacteria typically require no less than a 3-log reduction, yeast and mold no less than 1 log; falling short means the system is inadequate.

Step two: determine whether preservation is "both effective and mild" by measuring the minimum effective concentration — take the concentration that just passes the challenge test and add 20% as the formula usage level; above this amount there is no additional benefit to mildness.

VI. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How is a cosmetic's preservation judged as compliant? A: Compare against the public standard: total aerobic microbial count limit of 500 CFU/g, mold and yeast limit of 100 CFU/g, and heat-resistant coliforms, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa must not be detected, per the *Cosmetic Safety Technical Specifications*. In practice, write this into the acceptance terms — it's more reliable than a verbal agreement.

Q: If the same formula uses a new batch of raw materials, will preservation change? A: Yes. Changing suppliers means changing the set of variables — impurity profile, particle size distribution, and moisture content — so compatibility and stability validation must be redone; old parameters cannot be carried over directly. The reference criteria for verification are: type testing must be performed at least once a year on the full panel; routine factory release testing covers only total aerobic microbial count, mold and yeast, pH, appearance, and net content. Treating factory release testing as a full panel multiplies cost several times over and is unnecessary.

Q: If a cosmetic passes sample development but is unstable in mass production, where do you check first? A: Check first at the step where most organic acid preservatives rely on the undissociated form to be effective. Sample development is small-batch, single-batch; mass production amplifies errors in dispersion uniformity and temperature control.
