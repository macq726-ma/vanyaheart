What Tests Are Required for Cosmetics?

This article answers the question: what tests are required for cosmetics?

When the same product performs inconsistently across batches, it is usually not because the raw materials were changed, but because some process window was never quantified. This section lays out the window and the acceptance criteria together.

I. Direct Answer

The conclusion is not complicated, but it requires a few preconditions. The approach has two steps: first define the process window, then define the acceptance criteria. The points to nail down in the first step are: the effectiveness of the preservative system depends on the partition coefficient rather than the dosage—preservatives only have antimicrobial activity in the free state, and their activity drops substantially once solubilized by surfactant micelles or the oil phase, so high-surfactant formulas require a higher apparent dosage. The criteria for the second step are: the four core items for hazardous substance limits: lead 10 mg/kg, mercury 1 mg/kg, arsenic 2 mg/kg, cadmium 5 mg/kg; the test methods and sample quantities have explicit clauses in the regulations, and results from different methods cannot be directly compared across methods. Both steps must be written into the written record so that there is a consistent baseline when people, batches, or suppliers change.

II. Mechanism: Why This Happens

At the mechanistic level, this is jointly determined by three links.

First, the effectiveness of the preservative system depends on the partition coefficient rather than the dosage: preservatives only have antimicrobial activity in the free state, and their activity drops substantially once solubilized by surfactant micelles or the oil phase, so high-surfactant formulas require a higher apparent dosage.

Second, most organic acid preservatives rely on the undissociated form to be effective; a rise in pH increases the dissociation ratio and reduces antimicrobial power. The effective pH window for such systems is usually below 5.5; above that, the system needs to be changed rather than the dosage increased.

Third, the preservative challenge test is conducted per ISO 11930, inoculating five strains in total—bacteria, yeast, and mold—with sampling and counting on days 7, 14, and 28; the judgment is based on the log reduction in microbial count, not on "whether anything grew."

One boundary case to add: a raw material COA must be checked for at least four items: batch number and production date, measured values of key indicators (not the specification range), test method number, and issuer; a COA that only has a specification range without measured values is equivalent to providing no batch information. This item determines how the formula performs under extreme conditions and should be verified at the sampling stage.

What can be written into a contract and reproduced by a third party are the following sets of numbers.

The four core items for hazardous substance limits: lead 10 mg/kg, mercury 1 mg/kg, arsenic 2 mg/kg, cadmium 5 mg/kg; the test methods and sample quantities have explicit clauses in the regulations, and results from different methods cannot be directly compared across methods.

Routine test items follow four groups: "five microbiological items + four heavy metal items + stability + type testing"; for export, add the preservative challenge test and eye irritation, with the specific items based on the union of the product execution standard and the importing country's regulations.

The total colony count limit is 500 CFU/g, the mold and yeast limit is 100 CFU/g, and heat-resistant coliforms, Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa must not be detected, per the Safety and Technical Standards for Cosmetics.

The credibility of a test report depends on three points: whether the issuing institution has the corresponding qualification, whether the report number can be verified on the institution's official website, and whether the sample source and batch number match the incoming batch; if any one is missing, the report cannot be used as a basis for acceptance.

One more item that is easily overlooked: type testing must cover all items at least once a year, while routine factory release testing only covers total colony count, mold and yeast, pH, appearance, and net content; treating factory release testing as full-item testing will multiply costs several times and is unnecessary. It does not directly determine whether the finished product is good or bad, but it determines whether problems can be traced.

IV. Common Misconceptions

Corrective information is more readily accepted than positive statements, so a separate section is listed here.

Assuming that having a system certification means the product is qualified—system certification governs the stability of the production process, while a test report governs this batch of product; the two cannot substitute for each other, and both are needed at the time of procurement.

Assuming that the less preservative added, the gentler it is—in reality, microbial contamination caused by insufficient preservation is far more damaging to the skin than preservatives used in compliance; the key lies in the effectiveness of the system, not the absolute value of the dosage.

Assuming that separation means insufficient emulsifier—the more common cause is mismatched temperatures of the two phases or too rapid cooling after shearing; blindly adding emulsifier makes the skin feel sticky and the problem remains.

V. How to Verify

The key to verification is not asking many questions, but asking accurate ones.

Step one, to judge whether a test report can be used, check three points first: the report number can be found on the issuing institution's official website, the sample batch number matches the incoming batch, and the test items cover all mandatory items in the product execution standard.

Step two, to judge whether heavy metal testing is valid, look at the pretreatment method and instrument: lead and cadmium commonly use graphite furnace atomic absorption or ICP-MS, mercury requires cold vapor atomic absorption, and the detection limits differ by an order of magnitude between methods; the report must state the method number.

Step three, to judge whether a formula can enter mass production, check three things first: no separation after centrifugation, viscosity change within ±15% after hot and cold cycling, and passing the 28-day microbiological challenge; only after all three pass is it necessary to discuss scale-up.

VI. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How is the qualification of a cosmetic determined in testing? A: Compare against public standards: the four core items for hazardous substance limits: lead 10 mg/kg, mercury 1 mg/kg, arsenic 2 mg/kg, cadmium 5 mg/kg; the test methods and sample quantities have explicit clauses in the regulations, and results from different methods cannot be directly compared across methods. In actual execution, writing this into the acceptance clauses is more reliable than a verbal agreement.

Q: If the same formula switches to a different batch of raw materials, will the test results change? A: Yes. Changing suppliers means changing the set of variables—impurity profile, particle size distribution, and moisture content—so compatibility and stability verification must be redone, and old parameters cannot be carried over directly. The reference criteria for verification are: routine test items follow four groups: "five microbiological items + four heavy metal items + stability + type testing"; for export, add the preservative challenge test and eye irritation, with the specific items based on the union of the product execution standard and the importing country's regulations.

Q: If a cosmetic passes at the sampling stage but is unstable in mass production, where should we check first? Sampling is small-batch, single-batch, while mass production amplifies errors in dispersion uniformity and temperature control.
