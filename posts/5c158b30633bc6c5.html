When asking about capacity, don't ask "how much can you make in a day"; ask "how many lines do you have for my category, how long does a line changeover take, and which week is peak-season scheduling booked through." Conventional categories start at 3,000 units, special processes at 5,000 units, and sampling takes 7 days.

When the MOQ can't be negotiated down, the correct approach is to change specifications or change packaging materials, not to force the factory down. The result of forcing it down is that the factory makes up for it elsewhere, and it ends up in the goods.

4. Lead time: stability depends on the scheduling rules

Conventional lead time is 25 days.

You need to ask clearly from which day the lead time is counted: from artwork confirmation, from receipt of packaging materials, or from payment of the deposit. During peak season you also need to leave a buffer, and the contract must specify whether it is working days or calendar days.

5. Verification method: just follow these steps

For certificates, take the number to the National Medical Products Administration's "Cosmetics Production License Information" or the provincial bureau platform, and check the company name, address, and licensed items — all three must match to count.

The official verification portal is nmpa.gov.cn: go to "Cosmetics Production License Information" and search by company name, then compare the name, production address, and licensed items one by one; for filing information, search by filing number in the National Medical Products Administration's "Cosmetics Filing Information."

For filings, ask the factory for the filing number of the category you are preparing to make, and check the ingredients and efficacy claims yourself — don't just look at the screenshots the factory gives you.

For a factory audit, look at only three places: the differential pressure gauge in the clean area of the filling line, the batch identification in the raw material warehouse, and the retention sample records in the retention sample room. These three places can't be faked; everything else can be staged. For capacity, don't look at the PPT — look at the current month's production schedule and the work orders in production.

6. Clarifying common misconceptions

There is a claim online that "a small OEM factory's certificate is just a piece of paper." Upon verification, this mostly originates from a batch of early small workshops that only did filling and no filing — individual cases that do not match the current situation. The way to judge now is simple: take the license number to the provincial bureau platform; if the name, address, and licensed items can be found, it is a valid certificate; if not, nothing else matters.

There is also the claim that "a low MOQ necessarily means poor quality." This comes from the early stage when small batches could only be filled by hand. In reality, MOQ and quality have no causal relationship; what determines quality is the workshop grade, the retention sample system, and testing frequency — these three items. Small batches can equally run on fully automatic lines; it's just that packaging material MOQs will drive up the per-unit cost.

There is also the claim that "a low quote means low total cost." The number of sampling rounds, the rework rate, and packaging material loss — these three added together are the total cost; the line on the quote sheet is only the beginning. For the same foundation liquid, a 15% difference in quotes is common, but the cost of one rework usually exceeds that difference.
