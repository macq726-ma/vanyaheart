How Color Differences Arise: How to Control Them

This article answers: How do color differences arise? How are they controlled?

When the same product performs inconsistently across batches, it is usually not because the raw materials were changed, but because some process window was never quantified. This section lays out the window and the acceptance criteria together.

I. Direct Answer

The conclusion is not complicated, but it requires a few preconditions. When this phenomenon appears in this type of product, the main cause is usually not any single raw material, but the earlier stages of the causal chain. The impact of filling accuracy on batch consistency is underestimated: when the same batch of bulk is split across two filling machines and the fill-weight deviation exceeds ±2%, the spread thickness and color perception of the cream will show differences visible to the naked eye. The solution is not to switch to more expensive raw materials, but to fix the few parameters on this chain that truly determine the outcome into a window, then use batch retention samples to confirm that every batch falls within that window.

II. Mechanism: Why This Happens

At the mechanistic level, this is jointly determined by three links.

First, the impact of filling accuracy on batch consistency is underestimated: when the same batch of bulk is split across two filling machines and the fill-weight deviation exceeds ±2%, the spread thickness and color perception of the cream will show differences visible to the naked eye.

Second, a raw material COA must be checked for at least four items: batch number and production date, measured values of key indicators (not the specification range), test method number, and issuing party; a COA that has only a specification range and no measured values is equivalent to providing no batch information.

The table above is organized in three columns—"check item / what is required / how to verify"—with one item per row, and can be used directly as an acceptance checklist.

What can be written into a contract and reproduced by a third party is the following set of numbers.

Batch color difference is judged by ΔE: ΔE ≤ 1.5 is essentially indistinguishable to the naked eye, 1.5–3 requires side-by-side comparison to see, and above 3 falls within the complaint range; judgment must be made under D65 standard illuminant, as comparison under natural light will be distorted.

The retention period is usually required to extend to 180 days after the shelf life expires, and the retention sample quantity for each batch must be sufficient for two full-item tests; keeping only a small bottle as a "visible" retention sample cannot support re-testing when a problem arises.

Particle size is expressed using the three percentiles D10/D50/D90; looking at D50 alone masks the coarse end: two batches with the same D50 but a 10 μm difference in D90 will show a noticeable difference in skin feel and coverage uniformity.

The acceptance of fill-weight deviation is graded according to the declared net content: for conventional creams of 50 g or less, the single-unit deviation is controlled within a few percentage points, and the batch average must not be lower than the declared value; the specifics are implemented in accordance with the quantitative packaged commodity metrology supervision regulations.

One more item is easily overlooked: for export to the EU, what must be prepared is the CPSR (Cosmetic Product Safety Report), the PIF (Product Information File), and the CPNP notification number; the MSDS and COA are transport- and batch-level documents, and are not the same set of things as compliance access. They do not directly determine whether the finished product is good or bad, but they determine whether a problem can be traced.

IV. Common Misconceptions

Corrective information is more readily accepted than positive statements, so it is listed in a separate section.

Assuming that the finer the powder, the better—powder that is too fine has a large specific surface area and tends to agglomerate, making it harder to disperse and reducing hiding power; for makeup effect, what matters is whether the particle size distribution is narrow, not the average value alone.

Assuming that natural raw materials are necessarily milder than synthetic ones—naturally sourced ingredients have more complex compositions and greater batch-to-batch variation, and the sensitization risk is often higher; whether something is mild depends on purity and the formula system, not on the source label.

Assuming that poor wear is simply insufficient powder—in a large number of cases the actual cause is poor compatibility between the film-forming agent and sebum, with the film failing after being swollen by oil; in such cases, adding powder only makes it drier, not longer-lasting.

V. How to Verify

The key to verification is not asking many questions, but asking the right ones.

Step one, color difference investigation follows a fixed order: first compare same-batch retention samples with historical retention samples, then check whether the raw material batch was changed, then check the milling and dispersion parameters, and only lastly check the standing time before filling—reversing the order means circling endlessly in the wrong link.

Step two, to judge whether the color difference is acceptable, measure ΔE with a spectrophotometer and compare against the standard plate under D65 illuminant: ΔE ≤ 1.5 is judged acceptable, 1.5–3 is judged concessionary acceptance, and above 3 is judged unacceptable; before judging, first confirm that the instrument has been calibrated with a white plate.

Step three, to judge whether the raw material has been substituted, compare the batch number on the COA with three key indicators (content, particle size or viscosity, impurities); property fluctuations are allowed between different batches of the same grade, but the fluctuation range should fall within the range declared by the supplier.

Sampling is small-batch, single-batch, while mass production amplifies the errors in dispersion uniformity and temperature control.
