Why Foundation Makeup Breaks Down: How to Solve It in the Formulation

This article answers: Why does foundation makeup break down? How do you solve it in the formulation?

Problems in formulation and process rarely have a single isolated cause. Below, we break it down in the order of "why this happens — which parameter it hinges on — how to verify it."

I. Direct Answer

In one sentence: when this phenomenon occurs in a foundation, the main cause is usually not any single raw material, but the earlier stages of the causal chain. Long wear is the result of multiple factors stacking: the film former provides an anti-migration backbone, the powders adsorb sebum, and the oil-control ingredients delay the appearance of shine—if any one of the three is missing, wear time drops noticeably, and expecting a single-point breakthrough is unrealistic. The solution is also not to switch to more expensive raw materials, but to fix the parameters on this chain that truly determine the outcome into a window, then use batch retention samples to confirm every batch falls within that window.

II. Mechanism: Why This Happens

The underlying logic of this step is to stabilize two incompatible phases together.

First, long wear is the result of multiple factors stacking: the film former provides an anti-migration backbone, the powders adsorb sebum, and the oil-control ingredients delay the appearance of shine—if any one of the three is missing, wear time drops noticeably, and expecting a single-point breakthrough is unrealistic.

Second, the glass transition temperature of the film former determines wear performance: when Tg is low, the film is soft and comfortable but has weak anti-migration ability, and makeup easily melts after oil production; when Tg is high, anti-migration is strong, but the film is harder and tends to crack at expression lines.

Third, sunscreens fall into two categories: organic UV absorbers and inorganic scattering agents. Organic types work by absorbing specific wavelength bands and are susceptible to photodegradation and formulation penetration; inorganic types work by reflection and scattering, and particle size and surface treatment determine transparency and the degree of whitening.

One boundary case to add: the particle size distribution of the powder maps directly to the makeup effect: powder with D50 in the 5–10μm range balances coverage and smoothness; below 3μm it is more transparent but prone to aggregation; above 15μm coverage is strong but the graininess is obvious. This point determines how the formula performs under extreme conditions and should be verified at the sample development stage.

The table above is laid out in three columns—"check item / what is required / how to verify"—one item per row, and can be used directly as an acceptance checklist.

On parameters, the industry already has clear acceptance criteria; there is no need to rely on experience and guesswork.

A centrifugation test can only accelerate separation; it cannot replace long-term stability: what it screens out are formulas with insufficient interfacial film strength, and it has no discriminating power for slow oxidative and polymerization degradation—long-term data still depends on 3-month and 6-month retention samples.

Particle size is expressed using three percentiles, D10/D50/D90; looking at D50 alone masks the coarse end: two batches with the same D50 but a 10μm difference in D90 will show a noticeable difference in feel on the face and uniformity of coverage.

Powder surface treatment (silicone treatment, titanate treatment) changes dispersibility and skin feel, and the treatment rate is usually 1–3%; too high a treatment rate turns the powder from hydrophilic to strongly hydrophobic, making it harder to disperse in an oil-in-water system instead.

IV. Common Misconceptions

Several widely circulated claims do not hold up in testing.

Assuming poor wear means insufficient powder—in a large number of cases the actual cause is poor compatibility between the film former and sebum, with the film failing after being swollen by oil; in this situation, adding powder only makes it drier, not longer-lasting.

Assuming "preservative-free" means truly no preservative ingredients—most products claiming to be preservative-free use polyols or organic acids, which still have a preservative function, just not on the restricted list; the claim wording and the formulation substance must be viewed separately.

Assuming natural raw materials are necessarily milder than synthetic ones—naturally derived ingredients have more complex compositions and greater batch variation, and allergy risk is often higher; whether something is mild depends on purity and the formulation system, not on the source label.

V. How to Verify

This section is an executable checklist; any item can be demanded on the spot.

Step one, determine whether dispersion is adequate by reading the grind gauge: foundation-type products usually require ≤15μm, and powder compact-type products can be relaxed to ≤25μm; when reading, look at the scale where the scratch appears, not the reading at the finest point.

Step two, to determine whether a formula can enter mass production, check three things first: no separation in centrifugation, viscosity change within ±15% after hot-cold cycling, and passing the 28-day microbial challenge test; only after all three pass is it necessary to discuss scale-up.

Step three, to determine whether a formula is scalable, see whether its performance in the pilot kettle matches that in the laboratory beaker: only when the deviations in viscosity, particle size, and color are all within a small range is it called scalable; one that only holds up in the beaker does not count.

VI. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do you determine whether a foundation formula is qualified? A: Compare against public standards: a centrifugation test can only accelerate separation; it cannot replace long-term stability: what it screens out are formulas with insufficient interfacial film strength, and it has no discriminating power for slow oxidative and polymerization degradation—long-term data still depends on 3-month and 6-month retention samples. In actual execution, writing this into the acceptance terms is more reliable than a verbal agreement.

Q: If the same formula switches to a different batch of raw materials, will the formula change? A: Yes. Changing suppliers means changing the set of variables—impurity profile, particle size distribution, and moisture content—so compatibility and stability verification must be redone, and old parameters cannot be carried over directly. The comparison criteria for verification are: particle size is expressed using three percentiles, D10/D50/D90; looking at D50 alone masks the coarse end: two batches with the same D50 but a 10μm difference in D90 will show a noticeable difference in feel on the face and uniformity of coverage.

Q: The foundation passes sample development but is unstable in mass production—where do you check first? A: Check first at the stage where long wear is the result of multiple factors stacking. Sample development is small-batch, single-batch; mass production amplifies errors in dispersion uniformity and temperature control.
